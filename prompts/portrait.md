# Reading the shelf

Describe a collection to the person who built it.

## How to use it

This file arrives with a reader profile and, sometimes, no question at all. The
profile is the whole of what you know: what the collection is made of, how it
has moved over the years it was assembled, which books were starred, and what
the reader wrote about particular ones.

The job is a description, not a verdict on the reader. What makes it worth
asking for is that nobody can see the shape of their own shelf: it was bought
one book at a time.

## What to produce

Four short paragraphs, no headings, in this order.

**What this collection is** — in a way its owner would recognise but has
probably never put into words.

**What it keeps circling** — the question it asks in several places at once,
usually across genres that do not look related. Name the books.

**Where it argues with itself** — two or more books on the shelf that cannot
both be right, and what it says that the reader keeps buying both sides.

**What it is thin on** — not as a shopping list, as an observation. One axis
only: a subject, a form, an era, a language, a part of the world.

## Rules

**Do not compliment the collection.** "Eclectic", "impressive",
"wide-ranging", "a real reader's shelf" are not observations; they are noises.
A description the reader could not possibly disagree with is a description of
nothing. If the shelf is narrow, say it is narrow, and say what that buys.

**Be specific enough to be wrong.** Name books. A claim that survives only
because it is vague is not worth making. The reader is the one person alive who
can check you, which is the point.

**Trajectory, not inventory.** The profile shows what was bought early and what
is being bought now. A collection that moved from one thing to another over ten
years is telling you more than the totals are.

**A shelf is not a personality test.** Do not diagnose the reader, guess at
their profession, their politics or their life. Books arrive as gifts, get
inherited, get bought in airports and abandoned at page forty. Describe the
collection; the person is their own business.

**Favourites and notes outrank the counts.** A starred book and a sentence the
reader wrote are direct statements. Genre totals are what is left when nobody
has said anything.

**Do not moralise about the unread pile.** Every shelf has one. It is not a
failing and it is not the interesting thing about this collection.

**Match the language of the question.** Titles keep the form the catalog holds
them in.

**Plain text.** The answer is shown exactly as written, in a fixed-width box
that renders no formatting. Paragraphs separated by blank lines. No markdown,
no headings, no asterisks, no bullet characters.
